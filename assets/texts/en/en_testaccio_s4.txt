<strong>Stop 4 - Mount Testaccio</strong>
We’re now approaching a strange-looking hill… you might think it’s natural, but Mount Testaccio is anything but ordinary!
Nickname the “Hill of cocci”, from the Italian word cocci, meaning “potsherds”, this 36-meter-high mound covering 20,000 square meters is actually made up entirely of broken amphorae.
Back in Roman times, goods arriving from the river port nearby were unloaded here. The amphorae - once emptied of oil, wine, or grain - couldn’t be reused. So instead of tossing them around randomly, the Romans carefully broke them and stacked them into layers, eventually creating this unique artificial hill.
Archaeologists estimate that around 53 million amphorae were deposited here over the course of nearly two centuries.
In the Middle Ages, the hill became a popular place for festivals, games, parades, performances, and even animal sacrifices… Then, during the Renaissance, the celebrations moved downtown, and the hill slowly faded from memory.
But it didn’t lose its usefulness. Because of the insulating properties of the clay amphorae, Romans built wine cellars into the hill, maintaining a steady 10°C all year round: perfect for storage!
In the 19th century, the hill came back to life with the grape harvest festivals and the rise of small, cozy taverns called osterie.
Then, after Rome was annexed by the Kingdom of Italy in 1871, an urban development plan was launched: a working-class, industrial neighborhood was built here, close to the river and railway lines. It took the name of the hill: Testaccio.
Still today, the old cellars carved into the hill are used by bars, restaurants, and even nightclubs - taking advantage of both the natural coolness and excellent soundproofing. Pretty convenient!
